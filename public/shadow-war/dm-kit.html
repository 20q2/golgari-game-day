The Shadow War Dungeon Master's Field Kit

When the elder wyrms burned themselves out to seal the rift under Starbrook, that last flare of light threw a shadow across the whole of the north. The shadow stood up.

Section 01 — How it runs

Run Sheet

The premise, in the voice you'll use at the table

The rift is closed. The wyrms who closed it are dead or hollowed out. What came through the seam afterward doesn't march and doesn't besiege — it seeps. Nations that were allies last month don't answer letters this month. Three of its lieutenants are already inside the borders, testing the walls at night to find out what you've built.

You have twelve days before Noctyrix, the Long Dark arrives in person. Everything you build in those twelve days is the battlefield you fight him on.

Home is the Nova Kingdom. Its bastion is Starbrook — the town the party actually lives in. The walls, the workshop, the emplacements and the war table are all here, which is why every fortification level and every commissioned item happens in one place. When the kit says Starbrook it means the town; when it says the Nova Kingdom it means the region on the map, which can take Shadow like any other.

Eight other regions surround it. Three lieutenant lairs sit at the margins. That's twelve places on the board and twelve days to reach them.

Continuity with your one-shot

Optional but recommended: the party's Colossus, the Colossus, is what the elder wyrms poured themselves into when they had nothing left. That gives the new theme a reason to exist without making the campaign draconic — the light is spent, and the dark is what it left behind. If your group never played the original siege, cut the callback and the Colossus is simply the region's oldest weapon.

Note what this quietly relocates: the rift the wyrms died sealing was under Starbrook, and the pyre-field where they burned is the finale arena, half a day outside the town gates. If you'd rather that happened somewhere else in your world, move it — the only thing the kit needs is that the arena be inside the Nova Kingdom, so the Colossus's distance from home still matters.

How to run one day

Six steps, in this order, every single day.

  1. MUSTER 2 min · you talk

    Read the board out loud. Always in this order, so the players learn where to look:

    • The day number, and how many are left.
    • Shadow on the allied nations — name only what changed since yesterday, plus anything already at 3 or 4.
    • Where the Colossus is standing — and where the Standing Army is, and its Strength, if one is raised.
    • The three threat tracks — by name if the party knows the names, and as "the one at the wall / in the air / in your heads" if they don't.
    • What's in the Muster bowl.
    • Anything locked by last night. Say this last and say it plainly — a player who plans into a locked activity will rightly be annoyed.

    "It's Day Six. Six left. Who's doing what?"

  2. DECLARE 2 min · no dice yet

    Go round the table. Each character names one activity. Write all four down before anything is rolled.

    • Nobody sees a result and then re-plans. This is the whole game; hold that line politely and constantly.
    • Anyone spending their day on Assist declares it now, and who they're helping.
    • If they're launching an Assault, that consumes all four activities — skip step 3 and run the fight.

  3. RESOLVE 12 min · ~3 min each

    In declaration order. Four beats per character, every time:

    • They describe how they're actually doing it.
    • You name the skill and rule on any creative leverage — flavour, advantage, or automatic Great Success.
    • They roll. 10+ is 1 point, 25+ is 2, a natural 20 is always 2.
    • You mark it on the sheet immediately. Not at the end of the round — immediately, or you will lose one.

    On a failure, apply that activity's failure result now, then offer Push before moving on.

    If someone's activity is turning into a whole scene, cut to the roll. Four three-minute beats, not four scenes — the scenes are what Assault days are for.

  4. THE COLOSSUS & THE HOST 2 min · one order each

    The four of them agree on one order for the Colossus — MOVE, HOLD or STRIKE. No roll, and nothing to track but which region the token is in.

    Then, if a Standing Army is on the map, its owner alone gives it one order — MARCH, HOLD THE LINE or STRIKE. Don't let the table vote on this one; it is that player's piece and the point is that they get to command something.

    The easiest step in the day to skip and the one they'll most regret you skipping. Run it even when the answer is obviously "everything stays put and holds."

  5. NIGHT 8–12 min · roll in the open

    Read tonight's scheduled event out — the twelve nights run in fixed order, so you already know what tonight is and can have it ready. Then resolve in this order — it matters, because the players' choices can change what the Shadow gets to do:

    • Any choice the event offers them. Let them argue; don't let them stall.
    • Any roll it calls for — including a lieutenant night’s roll against DC 5 + (3 × track level), rolled openly.
    • Add or remove Shadow — the players place any marker they chose, you place the rest.
    • Advance any threat track the result advanced, then apply each advanced lieutenant's nightly push.
    • Last, from night 3 on: The Deepening. Whichever un-Fallen nation now carries the most Shadow gains 1 more. Resolve it last, every night, without discussion — it is weather, not an enemy.
    • Note what's locked or granted for tomorrow, in writing.

    If anyone burned a Cinder Kite at dawn they already know this result — roll it in the open anyway and enjoy watching them not be surprised. That feeling is what the item is for.

  6. CLOSE 1 min · write it down

    Tick the day tracker — the four activities taken, the night roll, and what it changed. Say out loud the one or two things carrying into tomorrow. Then start the next day.

The five things you will forget

  • The Colossus's order, and the Standing Army's.
  • Advancing a threat track after a lieutenant’s night breaks.
  • Telling them at Muster what's locked from last night.
  • Applying a real failure result instead of saying "nothing happens."
  • Marking the tracker before the next day starts.

Budget check

~28 minutes per ordinary day. Four days is about two hours, which leaves 30–50 minutes of a three-hour session for one Assault or mini-encounter.

An Assault day skips step 3 only. All four activities go into the fight, so it costs roughly the clock time of one and a half ordinary days. Still run steps 1, 2, 4, 5 and 6 around it — the Colossus still gets an order, and the night still happens after the fight.

About those red lines on your map

I've read the red boundary as the front the Shadow has already reached — which is why the Illva Kingdom opens at Shadow 2 while everything else is at 1 or 0. It's the best Day 1 hook available: the nation next door is visibly going, the players can see it going, and saving it costs actions they wanted for something else.

If the red lines were meant as something else — mountains, a river, a highway — set Illva to 0 and move that 2 onto Darkburg instead. Nothing downstream depends on it.

Say these out loud, whatever the handout says

  • "You get twelve days. There are no extra days."
  • "Everything you build shows up in the last fight. So does everything you don't."
  • "Your allies' powers work in any fight. Most of them are once, ever."
  • "One garrison order a round, and two Muster a round. However much you build."

People skim handouts. These four shape every decision they make for the rest of the campaign, so they get said aloud as well as printed.

Ask each player, before Day 1 begins

  • "Who have you lost?" Write the name down. Noctyrix's Hollow Voice speaks in it, his third lair action calls it out, and the Reliquary of Kept Names can protect it. You will not need to explain why that lands.
  • "Which two activities is your character actually good at?" It tells them to specialise and tells you where the party's holes are before the board finds them first.

Section 03 — The four activities

Activities & the Action Economy

The universal resolution

  • 10+  Success — 1 point in that activity.
  • 25+  Great Success — 2 points.
  • 9 or less  Failure — nothing, and the activity may bite.

At levels 17–19 a proficient specialist sits at +9 to +17, so 10+ is near-automatic for the right character and a coin-flip for the wrong one, while 25+ needs the specialist to roll about an 8. That's the intended shape: play to your strengths and you produce double; cover for a teammate and you produce half.

Push — the exhaustion valve

After a failed activity check, a character may Push: take one level of exhaustion and reroll. Straight from your original notes, where every Fortify check had an exhaustion alternative.

A long rest clears one level, so pushing on consecutive days stacks. Exhaustion carried into an Assault or into the finale is the real cost.

Activity 01Stealth · Investigation

Gather Intel

A Success buys one Stage; a Great Success buys two, on the same lieutenant or split across two of them. Stages must be taken in order. See the dossiers for exact reveal text — read it aloud, verbatim, and hand over the printed dossier sheet with that row filled in.

  • Stage I — Name. Unlocks the ability to Assault at all.
  • Stage II — Description & location. Removes the travel penalty on the Assault and reveals which of your three defences this lieutenant comes for.
  • Stage III — Secrets. Reveals the weakness, and unlocks that lieutenant's counter-weapon at the workshop.

The intel faucet is the night table, not this activity. Holding a lieutenant's night grants a free stage. A party that fortifies well barely needs to spend actions on Intel — which is exactly the strategic insight you want them to discover somewhere around Day 5.

A fourth intel target exists once all three lieutenants are at Stage III: the party learns Noctyrix's true name. See the finale.

Activity 02 — party-wideConsumes all four activities

Assault

Requires only Stage I on the target. One combat encounter, 30–45 minutes.

  • With Stage II or better: the party arrives rested and unseen. They act first.
  • With only Stage I: they spend the day finding the place. They arrive without a short rest, cannot spend Hit Dice beforehand, and the lieutenant acts first. Tell them this cost plainly before they commit — the choice should be informed.
  • With the Colossus standing at the lair: give it the STRIKE order and it joins the fight.
  • Allied benefits and Muster work here. Anything the party has secured can be spent on a lieutenant instead of saved for Noctyrix. Remind them once that the one-shots are once ever, then let them decide in peace.

A defeated lieutenant is gone from the finale, drops their artifact, and stops coming at night — which also means the party loses that lieutenant's free-intel faucet. Killing all three early is a real strategy with a real cost.

Killing one removes 1 Shadow from one nation adjacent to its lair — the party chooses which. Assaulting is map control as well as boss removal, and that fact should be discoverable rather than announced.

It stops the bleeding; it does not heal the wound. One marker, not one per neighbour — otherwise three kills scrub seven or eight Shadow off the board and a party that holds all three nights clears the map entirely. What a kill is really worth is the nightly push it switches off forever, which is enormous if that lieutenant's night broke and worth nothing at all if it held. Killing a lieutenant you already shut out is a finale decision, not a map decision — and letting the party work that out is one of the better realisations on offer.

Lieutenants do not escape. They have nowhere to be that is better than here and they know what their master does to the ones who come back. At 0 hit points they die. Never teleport one out at low HP — the entire preparation game is built on the promise that a lieutenant killed is a lieutenant gone, and breaking that once poisons the rest of the campaign.

Conversely: the party can retreat. If they withdraw, the lieutenant keeps its current hit points until the next long rest it gets — which is that same night, so it heals fully. The day is spent, they learn its full statblock for free, and nothing else is gained. Say this cost before they commit to the retreat.

At Threat 4 the lieutenant marches on Starbrook and the next day is a forced Assault. If the party declines it, the lieutenant sacks the town instead: lose 2 fortification levels of your choice and the Nova Kingdom gains 2 Shadow, then that lieutenant returns to Threat 3. Only one lieutenant may march at a time — any other at 4 waits its turn.

Activity 03Arcana · Artisan's tools · Religion

Commission Magic Item

One point buys one item from the workshop list. A Great Success yields two copies. Also the activity used to purify a captured artifact.

The workshop is a place on the map — it can be overcharged, cracked, or taken offline for a day by the night table. When that happens, say so at Muster.

Activity 04Athletics · Sleight of Hand · Religion / Arcana

Fortify Defenses

Three tracks, five levels each, all at Starbrook — the bastion of the Nova Kingdom and the only place on the map the party can fortify.

Name the track before you roll. A Success raises it 1 level; a Great Success raises it 2. All three start at 0 and cap at 5 — points past 5 are simply lost, so tell a player when they're about to waste a Great Success.

Levels are gained in order and every unlock is permanent once earned. When a night event destroys a level, the party loses the highest unlock in that track and can rebuild it later. If a track is already at 0 and would lose a level, the Nova Kingdom gains 1 Shadow instead — there's nothing left to break, so the dark comes inside.

The level number is also the defense. When one of the three lieutenant nights tests a track and you're resolving it abstractly, roll d20 for the enemy against DC 5 + (3 × track level). At level 0 they get through 80% of the time; at level 5, 5%. When you'd rather play the attack out, the level does the same job in a better way — it decides how much artillery the party has to answer with.

Garrison Actions — how fortifications are felt at the table

Everything the party builds arrives in one of two forms, and neither of them ever costs a character their turn:

  • Passives are simply true. Terrain, light, wards, nets. You describe them once when the fight starts and then apply them.
  • Garrison Actions are orders shouted at Starbrook's soldiers. On initiative 20, the party picks one — one, not one each — from everything they've unlocked, and it happens. No action, no reaction, no concentration. If Noctyrix also acts on 20, the garrison goes first: it's their town.

One per round is the whole balancing rule. A party with all three tracks at 5 has a rich menu and still only gets one order a round — so fortifying deep buys options, not extra damage. That's what keeps this from swallowing the fight.

A Standing Army standing where the fight is adds its own order to the same menu and competes for the same single slot. So does the Arcane Cannon Battery. There is only ever one order a round, no matter how much they own.

Several Garrison Actions are marked 1/battle. Those refresh between fights, not within one — so the Storm Rig can be called once against the sappers and again against the Long Dark.

Write the menu on an index card and hand it to the players. They should be reading their own options while you run the enemy's turn, not asking you what they own.

Level 5 in any track costs five successes — over 10% of the entire campaign's action economy. It should feel like a monument, and only one track reaching 5 is the normal outcome. Note also the shape of the tracks: Bulwark is tempo, Sky is control, Ward is denial. A party that reads that will specialise, and a party that specialises will find one of the three lieutenants much easier than the others.

Where all of this applies: at Starbrook, and nowhere else. Passives and Garrison Actions are live in every fight fought at the town — the wall encounters, a Threat-4 march, and the finale, which happens inside the walls.

They are not available on an Assault — with one exception. The Battle Tortoise carries the guns. With the Tortoise, the party may use Fire the Cannons, Loose the Ballistae and Scour anywhere on the map, one per round as usual. Nothing else travels — chains, slopes, watchfires and wards stay bolted to the town.

That is now the Tortoise's real job, and it makes Illva Kingdom the most consequential ally on the board for a party that fortified hard.

Activity 05Persuasion · Insight

Beseech Allies

Name one of the allied nations — any of them, at any distance, from Day 1. On a success, choose one:

  • Take their Special Benefit — a named, one-shot power for the finale.
  • Take 2 Muster — the finale intervention currency.
  • Push the dark back — remove 1 Shadow from that nation.

A Great Success takes two different options of the three — not the same one twice, so 25+ never means 4 Muster.

This one check is where the campaign's central question lives. The same roll either makes you stronger or stops the world getting worse, and it is never both unless you roll 25. Every time a player hesitates here, the design is working — let the pause happen and don't rescue them from it.

A nation at Shadow 3 (Corrupted) can still be beseeched, but only for Shadow removal — its benefit is unavailable until it's back to 2 or lower. A nation at Shadow 4 (Fallen) cannot be beseeched at all.

Activity 06 — requires a War RoomNo check · 1,000 gp

Raise or Reinforce an Army

For the player whose Bastion has a War Room. Their table ruling — about a thousand gold and a few days buys a force roughly a thousand strong, cavalry included — translates into this campaign as one activity and one new piece on the board.

No roll. Spend the activity and 1,000 gp; the host rides in at dawn. A Standing Army is not a thing you can fail to raise — the War Room owner has already done the hard part by building it.

  • The owner may take this activity, and so may anyone the owner lends the room to. The credit and the orders stay with whoever raised it.
  • One Standing Army on the map at a time, party-wide. If it is destroyed, another can be raised for another activity and another 1,000 gp.
  • The gold is not the cost. At level 17 a thousand gold is pocket change and everyone at the table knows it. The real cost is the day, and the fact that the host can die.

Reinforcing is this activity, not an order

If a Standing Army is already on the map, this activity restores 1 Strength for 1,000 gp instead of raising a new one — or gives +3 Muster if it is already at full Strength. There is no cap on how often; the muster rolls are long and the coin was never the problem.

Topping the army up costs a character a day. The army does not spend one of its own orders doing it, and it keeps marching, holding or striking while the paperwork happens somewhere behind it.

That is the real limiter, and it is a good one: every point of Strength bought back is an activity not spent on intel, walls, items or allies. So the activity is never wasted.

If a second player also has a War Room, don't double the Standing Armies — let them raise it instead when the first one falls, or give them the standing order on the existing Standing Army. The cap is what keeps this from unravelling the map; see the note under the Standing Army.

Section 04 — Answers you will need in the first ten minutes

Rulings

Every question four experienced players will ask on Day 1, answered in advance so you never have to invent a precedent under pressure.

The check itself

What is a "point"?

Never a bankable currency — it's the size of the result. One point is one intel stage, one fortification level, one standard item, one Beseech option. Points are spent the moment they're earned and nothing carries over to tomorrow.

Natural 20 and natural 1

A natural 20 is always at least a Great Success, whatever the modifier. A natural 1 is always a Failure. This is the only way an off-role character produces double, and it happens just often enough to be worth the gamble.

Advantage

Works normally and absolutely can push a roll to 25+. Where an ally, item, or night event grants advantage, it is a real shot at a Great Success, not just insurance.

Can two players take the same activity?

Yes, and results stack. Two characters both fortifying Bulwark can raise it 2–4 levels in one day. Two characters both beseeching the same nation is legal but capped: a nation can only give each of its three options once, ever.

Assist

A character may forgo their activity entirely to grant advantage on another character's check. Two actions for one advantaged roll is usually a bad trade — which is the point. It exists for the day the whole party needs one specific thing to land.

Do they get a long rest every night?

Yes, unless the night event says otherwise. Spell slots and Hit Dice reset, and one level of exhaustion clears. That's what makes Push sustainable in isolation and ruinous two days running.

Which exhaustion rules?

Use whichever edition your table already uses, but decide before Session 1 and say so. On 2014 rules, one level means disadvantage on ability checks — which makes Push a genuine gamble against tomorrow. On 2024 rules it's −2 to all d20 tests, which is milder; if you're on 2024, cap Push at two levels per character across the whole campaign so it stays a decision.

Creative leverage

The thing that makes this a D&D game rather than a spreadsheet: they will ask whether fabricate, sending, planar binding, or a favour owed to a guild changes the roll. Three tiers, applied consistently:

Flavour — no change

They describe how their character does it. Good narration, same check. "I use my noble background to open doors." Reward it with attention, not numbers.

Applicable — advantage, or +2

A specific resource genuinely suited to the task. Sending to coordinate a Beseech across a Shadowed border. Fabricate in the workshop. A familiar scouting a lair. Costs them the spell slot; give advantage.

Transformative — automatic Great Success

A resource that solves the activity rather than assisting it, and costs something real. Scrying or contact other plane aimed at a named lieutenant. Planar ally spent on a Beseech. Wish, obviously. Allow it, once per resource — the second scrying finds only what the enemy now expects.

Say yes, then charge for it. A 19th-level party should be able to break an activity open with the right spell. The cost is that they spent a slot and a day, and that the Shadow adapts — a transformative trick used twice stops being transformative.

The guns, and who fires them

Starbrook's artillery is never a character's action. It is a Garrison Action — an order, given at initiative 20, once per round for the whole party.

  • The crews are soldiers, not players. Nobody rolls to operate anything. The party names a target and the guns speak.
  • The attack bonus is fixed at +12 — it's the crew's, not the caller's, so it never scales with who is shouting. Against a Huge or larger creature a shot simply hits; a cannon does not miss a Gargantuan.
  • One order per round, total. Owning cannons and ballistae and scopes means choosing between them, not firing all three.
  • The Arcane Cannon Battery is a commissioned item rather than a fortification, but it behaves the same way: one more order on the menu, usable once per battle.
  • Guns cannot be destroyed for good. A wrecked emplacement is rebuilt free by morning — only the fortification level is precious.

Assorted

Do the characters travel? No.

The party has no position on the map. They work out of Starbrook and their reach is the war room, not their feet — sending stones, riders, the mage corps' relay, a spy network that was already in place before any of this started. That is why Beseech Allies works on TENADEEZ, three regions away, on Day 1.

Distance belongs to the Colossus and to Standing Armies, and to nothing else. They are the pieces that occupy space, and that is exactly what makes their orders the most argued-over two minutes of each day. If characters also had positions, the map would stop being about them.

The one exception is an Assault — the only time the party physically leaves. Travel there isn't tracked; it's already paid for by the activity costing all four of them, and by the Stage I penalty for not knowing where they're going. This is also why the Battle Tortoise matters: it's the only thing that carries the guns off home ground.

If a player wants to go in person — teleport to Mary's Land and make the case to the celestials face to face — say yes and give them the scene. It's still one Beseech activity and the same check. At these levels a character can be home for dinner, and a throne room is more fun than a letter. Just never let it become a second activity.

The "shadow" tag

Several items and effects say "shadow creature." It means any creature whose type line carries the (shadow) tag: Noctyrix, his Cast Shadows, all three lieutenants, and every cultist, thrall, and sapper in the campaign. If it serves the Long Dark, it's a shadow creature.

Attunement and copies

Nothing on the workshop list requires attunement unless it says so — the party is meant to stack this stuff across four characters, not budget slots. The three purified artifacts do require attunement, one each.

A Great Success on a standard item yields two copies. Copies are identical and independently used.

Major items cost 2 points

One Great Success buys one Major item, or two characters can each contribute a Success on the same day, or one character can bank a Success and finish it tomorrow — declare the Major item when the first point goes in.

When can allied benefits be used?

In any fight the party chooses — a lieutenant Assault, a night mini-encounter, or the finale. There are two kinds:

  • One-shots. Call the Storm, Divine Grace, the Night Sphere, Gravity Squeeze, Necrotic Tempest, and the Hour Unspent. Once ever, not once per fight. A storm spent on Khorvax is a storm that is not there for Noctyrix.
  • Standing assets. The Battle Tortoise, the Creation Troop's daily object, and Mary's Land's daily Taint removal. Never consumed — they simply work, every day and every fight. Outer Penn's extra activity sits between the two: a single use, but spent on the map rather than in a fight.

Muster is spendable in any fight too, at the same cap of two per round.

Section 06 — Pieces that are not characters

The Colossus & the Host

Two pieces on the board that nobody plays as a character. Each has one number, one order a day, and no dice ever. The Colossus belongs to the party; a Standing Army belongs to whoever raised it.

The Colossus — the whole piece in four lines

  • It has no hit points and cannot be destroyed. There is no stat to track. Nothing on the night table can break it.
  • It stands in one region at a time and starts at Starbrook.
  • Each day the party agrees on one order from the three below. No check, no roll.
  • Its only limit is where it is standing. That is the entire design — it is an enormous problem-solver that can only solve one region's problem at a time.

MOVE

One region along any line on the map, solid or dashed.

Everything expensive about the Colossus is here. Nothing else costs it days.

HOLD

Its region gains no Shadow tonight, from any source. Not reduced — none.

Absolute, because it has no other cost. One region a night is protected completely, and every other region is not.

STRIKE

It attacks whatever is where it stands.

  • In a nation: remove 1 Shadow. The only reliable way to pull somewhere back from Corrupted.
  • At a lair on an Assault day: it joins the fight for 60 damage a round to a target the players name, ignoring resistance, and it soaks one wave of minions each round.

One order, and where it's standing decides what it means.

The question you want them arguing about

Twelve orders, twelve places, three lairs. Walking it from Starbrook down to the Sunken Barrows and home again is six days — half the campaign. It is the most powerful thing they own and it is always in the wrong place.

Make the order collective, and say where it's standing at every Muster.

In the finale

The arena is Starbrook. If the Colossus is standing there on Day 12 it fights the entire battle — 60 damage a round and a soaked minion wave every round, for as long as the fight lasts. If it is not, it arrives one round later for every region it has to cross.

Three regions out means it walks in on round 4. Announce that without apology; walking it home on Day 12 was an order they could have given.

The Standing Army

Raised by a player with a War Room. Where the Colossus is indestructible and slow, an army is fast, expendable, and made of people.

The whole piece in four lines

  • Raised with the Raise or Reinforce an Army action. Roughly a thousand riders and foot.
  • One stat: Strength, starting at 3. At Strength 0 it is destroyed and a new one must be raised.
  • It stands in one region and starts wherever it was raised.
  • Its own order each day, given by whoever raised it — not by the party.

MARCH

One region along any line on the map. Costs nothing.

HOLD THE LINE

If its region would gain Shadow tonight, prevent 1 of it — and lose 1 Strength doing so.

It only costs Strength when it actually prevents something. An army holding a region nothing attacks has spent a quiet day and lost nothing.

STRIKE

Costs 1 Strength. It attacks where it stands: remove 1 Shadow from its nation, or — at a lair on an Assault day — it joins the fight, putting Send in the Riders on the garrison menu.

Armies pay for what they do. Every strike is three hundred people fewer.

Three orders, and none of them is reinforce — an army does not resupply itself. Buying Strength back is the commander's job, and it costs them a whole activity.

In a battle

An army standing where a fight happens adds one order to the garrison menu:

Send in the Riders. 4d10 to each of up to three enemies in a 60-ft line; each makes a DC 18 Str save or is knocked prone with its speed halved until the end of its next turn.

Sound the General Advance. Free action: disband the army outright. It is destroyed, and the party gains 2 Muster per remaining Strength.

It shares the one-order-per-round cap with everything else the garrison can do.

DM note — watch the Shadow budget

The campaign rests on roughly 12 Shadow arriving over 12 nights and two or three nations Falling. An army is a Shadow-prevention engine and reinforcement is unlimited, so the limiter is the party's action economy:

  • The first three Shadow it prevents are free — that is the Strength it was raised with. Every one after that costs a character's whole day to buy back.
  • So an army that prevents seven Shadow has eaten five activities — one to raise it and four to top it up — which is about a tenth of the campaign. That is a real trade against intel, walls, items and allies, and it is the trade that keeps this honest.
  • One army at a time, party-wide. That cap is still the important one.

If nothing is trending toward Falling, don't nerf the army — turn the other dial. Play night 10 more often, or let threat tracks push into two nations instead of one.

Balancing note

These are sized for four characters at 17–19 dealing roughly 250 damage per round as a party, aiming at a five-round fight inside a 40-minute window. Khorvax's regeneration and Veshra's regeneration are the tuning dials: if the party arrived with the right counter-weapon, the fight runs short and should — that's the reward. If they arrived blind, the regen is what makes them regret it.

These numbers assume no allied one-shot is spent here. A party that brings the Battle Tortoise, a storm and the Colossus will flatten a lieutenant, and that is the correct outcome — they paid for it with days, and they paid again by not having the storm at the end. Let it be a rout and do not add hit points to compensate.

Section 08 — The Shadow's turn

The Twelve Nights

Not a random table. Twelve nights in fixed order, four per session. Every one of them is a decision — not a thing that happens to the party, but a choice between two costs where they place the consequence on the board themselves.

Each session runs the same shape: open soft, escalate, spotlight a player, close on a lieutenant. Nights 3, 7 and 10 are Personal Nights. Nights 4, 8 and 12 are the session-enders and each carries a timing note. Three further events sit in reserve.

Make them place the marker

Where a night says a nation of the party's choice, do not choose for them and do not place the token yourself. Hand the marker to whoever argued loudest and make them put it down. A Shadow token the players placed on a neighbour they decided to sacrifice is worth ten that you assigned, and it is the entire reason this campaign works.

The three-tier ramp — how hard each session pushes

One number per session, and nothing else to remember. The party is level 17, then 18, then 19, and the board should feel like a different game in each tier: open, then expensive, then triage.

SessionNightsA bad outcome costsWhat the night is really asking

Two L18 5–8 2 Shadow What will you pay not to? The cost doubles, and the safe branch stops being free — from night 6 on, avoiding a night spends something: tomorrow's Fortify, a Beseech, the Colossus's order, 1 army Strength.
Three L19 9–12 3 Shadow What will you keep? Every night can be bought off with the Hold clause — and the list of things left to spend gets shorter every time they use it.

The Hold clause — nights 9 to 12 only

Every night in session three gains a third branch. Read it out as an option every time, in these words:

Hold it. Spend one of the following, and the Shadow that night would place does not land at all.

  • One fortification level, from any track, party's choice
  • The Colossus's order tomorrow — it stands still and does nothing
  • 1 Standing Army Strength
  • One unspent charge of an allied nation ability
  • One commissioned item, destroyed in the doing

One asset per night, and nothing is refunded. It buys off Shadow only — never Taint, exhaustion, a lost action, or anything the party volunteered for by taking a bargain. Night 12's terms are a decision, not an attack; they cannot be bought out of it.

This clause is the whole design of session three. The Shadow number barely matters — what matters is that four nights in a row ask which of your things is worth a region, and the menu is shorter each night. By night 11 a party that has been paying is choosing between the Hour Unspent and a nation, out loud, in front of each other. That is the game.

The dial you hold every single night

The Deepening lands on the un-Fallen nation carrying the most Shadow — and on a tied or clean board the tie-break is nearest a lair, then your choice. That choice, and nothing else, decides how the campaign ends.

  • Concentrate — the same nation, night after night — and it Falls. Guaranteed, at any volume.
  • Spread — rotate the target — and nothing Falls, ever, however much Shadow you pour onto the board.

So run it live. Pick your two doomed nations around night 6, feed them consistently, and rotate everything else. You will hit exactly two Falls with no arithmetic at all, and the table will never know it wasn't the dice.

Pick nations the party has under-invested in. A Fall should read as the bill for a choice they made, not a tax you levied.

Three rules that keep this feeling good rather than punishing

  • Never take something they built without offering a choice about it. Losing a Bulwark level to a failed roll feels like theft. Choosing to burn that level to save TENADEEZ feels like a war. Identical loss, opposite feeling — and it is the entire reason the Hold clause is spend-or-lose rather than lose.
  • Telegraph every Fall three nights out. Name the nation at Muster every morning it is in danger. They need time to try, fail, and know they chose something else. A nation that Falls without warning is bookkeeping; one they watched slip away is the story they tell afterwards.
  • Let them keep whatever they never spend. The assets that survive session three intact are the reward for triaging well, and they are what makes the finale feel earned. Do not write a night that confiscates those.

What It Left Behind

A dead lieutenant does not take its household with it. Whatever it kept in order down there has spent days without an order and is coming up through the roots and the water table, feral and directionless and spreading.

Every nation adjacent to that dead lieutenant's lair gains 1 Shadow — two nations for the Hollow Choir or the Mirror Spire, three for the Sunken Barrows. Place them yourself, and name each nation aloud as you do.

It is entirely preventable, but only by presence. Any of those nations that currently holds the Colossus, the Standing Army, or a Warding Cairn takes nothing at all.

This is the night that punishes going home. A party that marches everything back to Starbrook the morning after a kill will watch two or three nations darken for it, and a party that left the army forward will not. That lesson is worth more than the Shadow, and it teaches itself.

Keeping the board frightening

The Deepening — the spread walls cannot stop

From night 3, at the end of every night, the un-Fallen nation carrying the most Shadow gains 1 more — and from night 9 it gains 2. Ties go to whichever is nearest a lair, then your choice. No roll, no save, and no fortification level touches it — this is the land turning, not an army arriving.

Why it doubles. Removal is one Shadow per source per day, and every source stands in exactly one place. A nation taking 2 a night can only be held by parking the Colossus and the Standing Army and a Beseech on it — which means abandoning the rest of the map to save one region. From night 9 the board stops being an arithmetic problem and becomes a triage problem. See the three-tier ramp.

Fourteen Shadow across ten nights, and it always lands on whoever is already worst off — so it drives a nation toward Falling rather than bruising everyone evenly. The only answers are the ones that remove Shadow: Colossus STRIKE, Beseech spent on removal, a Standing Army STRIKE, a Warding Cairn or a Scroll of Hallowing.

Why it exists. Without it the campaign has a win button. Every nightly Shadow used to come from lieutenant threat tracks, so a party that held all three nights stopped the spread entirely — and maxing all three tracks costs 15 of roughly 48 points, which is affordable. They would have saved the whole map by night 4 and spent eight days watching nothing happen.

Volume does not decide the outcome — targeting does. Seventeen Shadow spread evenly across eight nations at 4 capacity Falls nothing at all; the same seventeen concentrated Falls four. Every number above is a ceiling, and the dial is where you set it. Two Falls is the target for a party playing well, and you steer to it night by night rather than computing it.

The first nation to Fall is the one that decides the campaign. A Fallen nation pushes 1 Shadow into a random neighbour every remaining night, so one that Falls on night 6 is worth six more Shadow and one that Falls on night 10 is worth two. That is why the two columns diverge so hard: the sloppy party isn't taking more hits, it is taking its hits earlier, and the board compounds them.

The Deepening skips Fallen nations and moves to the next-worst survivor. Losing one does not relieve the pressure — it redirects it onto whoever is now most fragile, while the Fallen nation starts spreading on its own. There is no point at which conceding a region buys peace.

Three habits that keep it real

  • Announce spread before actions, not after. At Muster, say which nations will take a Shadow tonight from the threat tracks. They then spend the day knowing, which converts a passive drip into a daily emergency.
  • Never move a marker yourself when a player chose it. Hand it over.
  • Say the name of the nation every time. Not "a neighbour" — Darkburg. Nations that are named get mourned; nations that are "a region" get spent.

If they are somehow running clean into session three — every night held, nothing Fallen — do not invent a disaster. Swap a reserve night in for night 9 and let them have earned it. They will need the goodwill in the finale.

Why the lieutenant rotates by track

One lieutenant night per session, and a different track each time — Bulwark, then Sky, then Ward. Four good things fall out of that:

  • All three fortification tracks matter. A party that poured everything into Bulwark will feel nights 8 and 11 personally.
  • Each lieutenant gets a spotlight, so all three read as characters rather than a list of three.
  • Each wall encounter gets its night. That's why there are exactly three of them, one per track.
  • By session two the players can predict it — and that isn't a leak, it's the whole game. They get to spend a day fortifying the track they know is about to be tested.

One night each, so a break costs two steps

With a fixed schedule each lieutenant comes exactly once. A single step per break would cap every track at 1 and no lieutenant would reach Empowered — so a broken night advances two steps, straight to Threat 2 and its Empowered trait.

That makes the one roll matter enormously, which is correct: it is the only night that lieutenant gets, and the party had days of warning. Night 12 adds a third step to anyone still alive, so a lieutenant they ignored entirely arrives at the finale Ascendant, with +100 HP.

Personal Nights — the three callbacks

Three nights that belong to one player each, drawn from things their characters did before this war started. They do not sit on the d12, and there's a reason for that.

How they fire

They are nights 3, 7 and 10, already built into the schedule; move one a night either way if the table needs it. No scheduled event fires on those nights. Three callbacks, three sessions — all of them land.

None of them is free. A callback that is pure profit is a cutscene. Each of these three hands the player something genuinely powerful and then asks what it is worth — in coin, in walls, in Shadow, or in Taint. The gift lands first and the bill comes second, always in that order.

The schedule picks the night; the die picks whose it is. Roll d3, then d2, then take whoever is left. Nobody at the table knows who is about to get their moment, which is the part that actually needs to be random.

Why not just put them on the table

Because they'd mostly never happen. A 13th slot on a d12 fires about 27% per session — expected total across twelve nights is under one. Most groups would see a single callback and some would see none at all.

The other reason is timing. A callback landing on the night a player is already having a great session is worth a fraction of the same callback landing when they've failed three rolls running. Hold them and play them.

Use character names at the table, not player names. These are written with the players' names for your convenience because that's how you'll remember whose is whose. Nobody in Starbrook has heard of Brandon.

The three wall encounters

If that lieutenant is already dead, none of this happens — run one of the two replacement nights instead.

One per fortification track. Each is the played-out version of that lieutenant's night — the same attack, run as a fight instead of a die roll, so the party actually gets to fire the things they built.

When to play one instead of rolling

  • Only if the track being tested is at level 1 or higher. At level 0 they own no Garrison Actions and there is nothing to play — roll the d20 and let them lose the abstract way.
  • Only if you have 20 spare minutes. The DC 5 + (3 × level) roll is always available and always legitimate. Nothing downstream cares which method you used.
  • Once per session is plenty. Three across the campaign — one per track — is the target, so each track gets its showcase.

The fortification level is the difficulty setting and needs no other maths. At Bulwark 1 the party has two cannons and will struggle. At Bulwark 5 they have cannons, a slope, chains, watchfires and the Deepwork, and they will hold comfortably. That difference is the reward.

Breaches — the one clock all three use

The defence has 10 Breaches of capacity. Enemies fill the count; the party is trying to run the clock out before it fills. Track it in tallies beside the initiative order where everyone can see — a visible number the enemy is filling is worth three rounds of description.

At 10 Breaches the walls give: lose 1 level in the track that was tested and the Nova Kingdom gains 1 Shadow. Below 10 the party holds: the attack fails, that lieutenant's threat track does not advance, and the party learns one free intel stage on them — exactly as though they'd made the abstract roll.

Breaches reset to 0 by morning. The masons work all night and nobody thanks them.

Sketching the battlefield in ninety seconds

None of these needs a prepared map. On a blank grid, for every one of them:

  • One straight line across the lower third. That's the wall. Everything above it is outside.
  • Two or three small boxes on the line. Cannon and ballista positions. The party stands on or behind the wall.
  • A dashed line across the top. Where the enemy comes from, and roughly how far out.

That is the whole map. Each encounter below adds one extra thing to draw, and no more. If you find yourself drawing buildings, stop.

Wall Encounter IBulwark · Khorvax

Shadow Sappers at the Wall

Also draw: four X marks along the dashed line. Those are where the packs come out of the dark.

Four sapper packs carrying charges. Treat each pack as one creature — AC 14, 100 HP, speed 40, immune to fear — because tracking forty individual bodies will eat your session.

  • Arrival: pack A reaches the wall in round 1, B in round 2, C in 3, D in 4. Four rounds total.
  • At the wall: each pack sets 2 Breaches at the end of the round. Left alone that's 20, so the party must destroy roughly half of what arrives.
  • If engaged in melee: a pack attacks instead of planting — +7, 4d6 slashing to one creature within 5 ft. Standing in front of the wall genuinely works, and genuinely hurts.
  • Area damage hits a pack in full.

Fire the Cannons kills most of a pack in one order. The Slope costs them a round of approach. Watchfires mean they never get to hide. This is the encounter where Bulwark is obviously, loudly correct.

Held: +3 Muster — the survivors decide they'd rather be on this side of the wall, and they know where the charges are buried.

Wall Encounter IISky · Seravaine

The Kettle of Wings

Also draw: nothing on the ground. Write an altitude number beside each flier instead — 60 ft, 120 ft. That's the only extra bookkeeping this fight needs.

Seravaine doesn't besiege. She sends her flock to take people. Six stitched wings — AC 16, 70 HP, fly 80 ft (hover), immune to fear — circle above the walls out of reach and stoop one at a time.

  • Stoop: a wing dives, makes one attack at +10 for 3d8 against a defender, and on a hit grapples and climbs 40 ft with them. It flies off at the end of its next turn.
  • Each defender carried away is 1 Breach. They are named soldiers of Starbrook; say a name each time, and if the party has a Reliquary of Kept Names, that lands even harder.
  • A grappled PC can escape (DC 17), and anyone can shoot the wing carrying them — it drops what it holds when it dies. Falling is the party's problem.
  • They will not land. A party with no ranged answer at all simply loses this one, and should.

Loose the Ballistae halves a flier's speed and stops it disengaging — which strands it in reach. Fog Nets do that to all six, permanently, and turn the fight into a slaughter. Skywatch means they never get a free first stoop.

Held: +3 Muster, and a wing brought down intact is worth one free intel stage on Seravaine — the thing was stitched from someone recognisable.

Wall Encounter IIIWard · Veshra

The Choir Beneath

Also draw: three small circles anywhere inside the walls, face-down or unlabelled. Those are the anchors. The party doesn't know which is which until they look.

There is nothing to fight for the first round. Veshra has planted three anchors — objects, or people who don't know what they're carrying — and through them the whole garrison begins speaking in one voice.

  • At initiative 20, each surviving anchor speaks. Every creature inside the walls makes a DC 19 Wis save. Each anchor whose save the party collectively fails is 1 Breach — one more piece of the garrison turned.
  • Anchors are objects: AC 15, 40 HP, immune to psychic. Destroying one silences it permanently. Three anchors, so three rounds of work if they find them fast.
  • Finding them is the fight. An anchor is invisible and inaudible until a creature succeeds on a DC 20 Investigation check within 30 ft of it, or until something reveals it.
  • The turned: at 5 Breaches, four hollowed soldiers (AC 15, 30 HP) attack the party. They can be restrained rather than killed, and a player who thinks to do that should be rewarded for it.

Truesight Lattice reveals all three anchors instantly and skips the entire search — an absurd, wonderful payoff for Ward 4. Scour can hunt a suspected square without anyone walking into it. Consecration burns the hollowed as they close. Dampen negates one anchor's voice outright.

Held: +3 Muster, and the anchors were addressed to someone — one free intel stage on Veshra.

Do not run two of these in one session. Each is 20–30 minutes and each replaces a night event, so a session with a wall encounter has room for four days and nothing else. Three across the campaign, one per track, spaced one per session — that's the shape.

Section 09 — Allies

Allied Nations

Eight of the nine from your notes, mapped onto the regions you drew and ordered by how far the Colossus has to walk. Every Special Benefit has been converted to work in a four-player boss fight rather than a mass-combat siege — they are one-shot powers the players declare, not units on a grid. The Land of Magic is the one I cut: its arcane cannons were the most redundant thing on the list next to the emplacements the party already builds, so they became a Major workshop item instead of a ninth nation. Nothing was lost and there is one less region to track.

Muster — what "a flat addition to the army" means now

Spendable in any fight — Assault, mini-encounter, or finale. There is no mass combat, so armies are a currency of interventions. Each nation gives 2 Muster instead of its benefit. During the finale, spend Muster (maximum 2 per round, no action required, called by any conscious character) on:

  • Intercept — a soldier steps in front of an attack that has already hit. It misses instead. Works on anything with an attack roll, including a legendary action or Unmaking Touch.
  • Field Medic — a character at 0 hit points is dragged clear, stabilised, and stands with half their hit point maximum. They lose no turn to it.
  • Haul Them Out — four soldiers grab an ally and pull. Move them up to 30 ft to a space you choose and end one of grappled, restrained or prone on them. Khorvax grapples and reels; the second lair action makes the ground grab. This is the answer to both.
  • Rally — a hundred voices shout one name. One ally rerolls a failed saving throw against fear, charm, or psychic damage. Hollow Voice, Whisper, Eyebite, Frightful Presence.
  • Break Ranks — one minion is removed from the field entirely: a cultist, a sapper, a thrall, a wight, a stitched wing. Not an Aspect, not a lieutenant, not Noctyrix — if it has a statblock in this kit, it is not a minion.

Notice what is not on that list: damage. Four characters at level 20 already produce more of it than any garrison could add — a volley worth spending a charge on would have to out-damage a player's whole turn, and then the soldiers would be the heroes. Everything here instead does something the party cannot do for themselves: unmake a hit that landed, get someone up, break a hold, refuse a compulsion, delete a body.

That is also what makes Muster worth hoarding. These are not resources, they are saved moments — and a party that has been sitting on six of them since Day 3 will spend three in one round when it finally matters, which is exactly the scene you want.

Cap the spend at 2 per round or Muster becomes a wall of hit points and the finale stops being tense. Say the cap out loud in Session 1 so they plan around it.

A Standing Army can also be disbanded mid-battle for 2 Muster per remaining Strength — the one way the party can turn an army into interventions at the exact moment they need them.

1 day out · Nova · Outer Penn · Darkburg

The Western Frontier

Mountain and open country west of the Kingdom, never properly governed, where the weather has opinions and elementals wander loose through it.

Call the Storm. Once, in any one fight. Their elementalists steer a storm over the whole battlefield for 3 rounds. It is steered, so it spares the party — say that once and the question never comes up again.

While it blows, three things are true of the whole field:

  • Nothing can hide and nothing gains concealment. Noctyrix loses Cast By Light's cover entirely — the rain shows the shape of him.
  • Flying enemies move at half speed and cannot hover.
  • Every enemy makes a DC 18 Con save at the start of its turn. On a failure it takes the rider below and has disadvantage on all attack rolls until the end of its turn.

Choose one element as it's called:

  • Ice — the field is difficult terrain for enemies; a failed save also halves the creature's speed.
  • Flaywind — a failed save also blinds until the end of that creature's turn.
  • Flame — a failed save also deals 6d6 fire.
  • Acid — a failed save also deals 6d6 acid and pits armour: −2 AC until the end of its next turn.

A creature resistant to the chosen element ignores the storm entirely and sees clearly through it — so choose knowing what you're fighting. Against Seravaine, half speed and no hovering is worth more than any of the four riders.

1 day out · Nova · Outer Penn · Illva · Mirror Spire

Mary's Land

The old coastal realm across the northern water. Celestials keep a watch-house there. Formal, devout, and grieving — they buried Seravaine themselves, with full honours.

Divine Grace. Once, in any one fight, no action required. Every ally within 200 ft regains 60 HP, ends one condition — and cannot be reduced below 1 hit point until the end of the round.

"Ally" includes the Colossus, the Battle Tortoise, a Standing Army, and any risen shadow-hunter — anything on their side with hit points.

That last clause is what makes this interesting rather than a very large potion. It is not a heal, it is one round in which nobody dies, and the party has to decide which round that is.

And every day from the moment they're secured: Mary's Land removes 1 Taint from one character, free — no activity, no travel, no check. If Seravaine is still alive they will also tell the party who she used to be: Stage II intel, for nothing.

Structurally the most important nation on the board, because it is the only renewable answer to Taint. Its lieutenant is two days away and it opens at Shadow 1.

1 day out · Nova · Mary's Land · Sova · Mirror Spire

Illva Kingdom

The region's other crown, immediately south. Beast-tamers and heavy cavalry, the most conventional military power anyone has — and already inside the red line.

The Battle Tortoise. A living fortress, 40 ft across. AC 20 · HP 300 · speed 20.

  • It moves itself at the top of each round, up to 20 ft in a direction the party names. Nobody spends an action driving it.
  • It has no attacks. It is cover and a firing platform, nothing else.
  • Anyone inside has three-quarters cover — +5 AC and +5 to Dex saves — against anything outside, and can fire emplacements or cast out through the ports without giving that up.
  • It carries the guns. While aboard, the party may use Fire the Cannons, Loose the Ballistae, Scour and the Arcane Cannon Battery as Garrison Actions anywhere on the map — the only way any fortification leaves Starbrook.
  • At 0 HP it stops. It doesn't die — it becomes a 40-ft ruin that still grants half cover and still blocks movement.

The best pick for a party that fortified heavily, and it sits on the most endangered nation on the map. That tension is the whole campaign in one card.

2 days out · Western Frontier · Mary's Land · Hollow Choir

Outer Penn

Drawn outside the border on your map because it is only partly inside time. Their chronurgists answer letters they have not received yet. Veshra has put herself between them and everyone else on purpose.

The Hour Unspent. Once, in any one fight, no action required and no roll: the party takes an extra round. Every character immediately takes a full turn in initiative order. No enemy acts, no lair action fires, and no enemy may take a reaction or a legendary action during it. Then play resumes exactly where it stopped.

And once during the twelve days: one character takes two activities in a single day. Outer Penn lends them a morning that hasn't happened yet.

Why this and not foresight. Nothing else on the list gives the party tempo — every other ally hands them area control, protection, a platform, or a shutdown. An extra round is the one thing a level-20 party genuinely cannot buy anywhere else, and it is exactly what chronurgy is for.

It is also the only ally power that scales with how good the party already is, which makes it the strongest pick for a well-built party and the least useful for a floundering one. That is a fair way for a reward to behave.

Note the resonance with the Hollow Hour, Veshra's artifact — the same magic at a smaller scale. She lairs next door to Outer Penn and she means to take this from them. If the party works that out on their own, let them feel clever.

2 days out · Western Frontier · Sova · TENADEEZ · Sunken Barrows

Darkburg

A low, lamp-lit city in the western hills whose people have never cared for the sun and have built accordingly. Everything there is engineered for the dark.

The Night Sphere. Artificial night over an entire region. Read this twice: Noctyrix is a shadow, and a shadow needs light to be cast. Inside the Sphere there is nothing for him to be a shadow of — he cannot Shadow Step, cannot hide, cannot reform his Cast Shadows, and gains no concealment from Cast By Light.

Activation: any character's action, once ever, and it lasts the rest of that fight. It is not a light effect, so Long Night cannot snuff it and no spell dispels it.

It works on the lieutenants too, and hard. All three of them move or defend with shadow, so all three lose something they rely on: Khorvax can no longer sink and surface through its own fog and the fog stops obscuring; Seravaine cannot use Borrowed Shape at all and her mount never reforms; Veshra loses her Step and cannot cast mirror image.

The trade is real, not a freebie: with no bright light anywhere, Noctyrix is also never vulnerable to radiant. The Sphere kills mobility; Watchfires and the Lantern kill durability. A party can't have both, and working that out is the best strategic conversation the campaign offers. Don't hint — let Stage III intel or a clever player find it.

2 days out · Illva · Darkburg · NoCA

Sova Region

The southeastern lowlands, where a planar seam left the local mages practising graviturgy and holding a casual attitude toward the direction "up".

Gravity Squeeze. Once, in any one fight. A 200-ft-radius field for 3 rounds, centred wherever the party says. The Sova mages hold it open, so allied creatures are unaffected.

  • At the start of its turn, every enemy inside is dragged 20 ft toward the centre. No save — a Gargantuan creature is dragged 10 ft instead, but it still moves.
  • Flying enemies are pulled to the ground and land prone.
  • Moving away from the centre costs an enemy double movement.
  • At the end of each round, every enemy within 10 ft of the centre takes 6d10 force as the press crushes inward.

No save is the whole point. Noctyrix has +16 to every saving throw and would simply shrug off anything that offered him one; this drags him anyway. It is the party's answer to a boss who will not stand still, to a flier who wants distance, and to a minion wave arriving at the field's edge.

Section 11 — Optional module

Taint

The shadow mechanic, on the characters themselves rather than only on the map. Entirely removable — nothing else in the kit breaks without it — but it's what makes the theme land personally instead of geographically.

The premise: the dark is helpful. That's the whole design. Taint is never purely a penalty, or players will simply avoid every choice that grants it and the module does nothing. It gives before it takes.

Any Taint

Each Taint you carry grants one separate use per day of +2 to a d20 roll, declared after the roll. Three Taint means three +2s a day, not +6 on one roll. It always works. It always feels good.

At 2 Taint

Disadvantage on saving throws against Noctyrix's abilities and against anything with the shadow tag.

At 3 Taint

Once during the finale, the DM may spend your reaction — you simply don't have it when you reach for it.

At 4 Taint

Once during the finale, Noctyrix may compel one attack from you against an ally of your choice, no save. Tell the player this the moment they hit 4, and let them decide whether to spend a day clearing it.

Cap at 4. A character at 4 who takes more Taint instead loses their +2 pool for the rest of the campaign — the dark stops bargaining and starts owning.

Sources

  • Turning away the refugees (Night 5) — one character, party's choice.
  • Sleeping through Veshra's whispers rather than taking exhaustion (Night 8).
  • Failing the save on Night 12.
  • Every use of an unpurified artifact.
  • A failed purification attempt.
  • Dropping to 0 HP while TENADEEZ is Corrupted.
  • Failing the save against Noctyrix's Hollow Voice, in the finale itself.

Removal

  • Greater restoration — always works, one Taint. Your cleric now has a war job.
  • A Vial of Wyrmblood.
  • Mary's Land, once per day and free, from the moment they're secured.

Why this is the right shape for the theme. Corruption on the map is something that happens to the region. Taint is something the players choose, one small reasonable trade at a time, and by Day 12 they are carrying the Shadow Lord into his own boss fight. You never have to say any of that out loud. The mechanic says it.

Section 12 — Session four

The Long Dark

One session, one fight, level 20. No activities, no map, no night phase. Open by reading the Prep Ledger aloud — every line of it is something they chose.

The state they arrive in

  • Level 20, after a full long rest: every slot, every Hit Die, every daily item charge, no exhaustion.
  • Taint does not clear. It is the one thing a night's sleep doesn't touch, and this is the fight where its thresholds fire.
  • Muster is capped at 2 spends per round, no action required, any character may declare them.
  • Every allied benefit they have not already spent — one-shots are once ever, and several may have gone on the lieutenants. Standing assets are all still here. Each must be declared aloud by a character who is conscious.
  • Every passive is live and every Garrison Action is available — one order per round at initiative 20, before Noctyrix's lair action. The 1/battle orders have all refreshed.

The arena

Starbrook itself. He comes to their walls — there was never anywhere else he was going. The fight happens in the burned quarter, the scorched ground above the sealed rift, where twelve years of glass lies underfoot and a light still comes up out of the earth that nothing has been able to put out. Standing in that light, thrown fifty feet up the wall of the keep, his shadow.

Everything they fortified is here, because here is what they fortified. Open the fight by handing them the Garrison Action card and letting them read what they own — and note that the Colossus arrives on round 1 only if they walked it home.

The light is the trap and the weapon both. He exists because it does. Players who work that out have several ways to use it, and the Night Sphere is the sharpest.

Structure — the Tiamat shape your group already liked

Noctyrix plus three Cast Shadows, each rolling its own initiative. Four things acting per round, which keeps four players busy without a wall of minions. Use tallies for the hit points. Please.

The true name

If the party reached Stage III on all three lieutenants, they learn what he was called when he was still a thing that cast shadows instead of being one: AMUN-SEL. He was the light. This is the light's shadow, and it has been walking around for twelve years pretending to be a demon lord.

Once per fight, a character may speak it. Noctyrix loses all Legendary Resistance and cannot Shadow Step until the end of his next turn, and every creature on the field learns what he is.

This is the biggest single reward in the campaign and it is gated behind nine intel points. Make sure the players know a fourth intel target exists by Session 2 — the goal is a hard choice, not a missed secret.

Traits

  • Cast Shadows. Three Aspects act on their own initiative. AC 18, 150 HP each, half his damage, no legendary actions.   • Shadow Step. As part of any move, he teleports between any two shadows on the field. Shut down by the Night Sphere, and blocked into any lit space by Watchfires.
  • Cast By Light. While Noctyrix is in bright light he is vulnerable to radiant damage. In dim light or darkness he has three-quarters cover instead. The whole tactical puzzle is: keep him lit, and keep his shadows from coming back. Exception: inside the Night Sphere he gets neither — no cover, no vulnerability. There is no light for him to hide between and none to burn him.
  • Unbearable Presence. A creature that ends its turn within 20 ft takes 4d10 psychic, no save. Do not let the melee characters simply stand there.

Actions

  • Multiattack. Three Unmaking Touches, or one Touch and Hollow Voice.
  • Unmaking Touch. +17 to hit, reach 20 ft, 4d10+10 necrotic, and the target's maximum hit points are reduced by the damage dealt until a long rest.
  • Hollow Voice (recharge 5–6). 60-ft cone, DC 24 Wis, 12d8 psychic and 1 Taint, half damage and no Taint on a success. He speaks in the voice of someone each target has lost — ask them who, in Session 1, and write it down.
  • Long Night (1/day). Every light source within 300 ft is snuffed, including magical ones of 6th level or lower, for 3 rounds. If the party has no answer to this, the fight tilts hard — that's the point of the answers existing.

Lair actions — in fixed order, initiative 20, after the garrison

  • 1. The dark drinks. All light sources drop one step (bright to dim, dim to dark) until the next lair action.
  • 2. Grasping shadows. Every creature's own shadow reaches up. DC 22 Dex, 10d6 necrotic and restrained until the end of its turn, half and no restraint on a success.
  • 3. The names of the dead. Every creature: DC 22 Wis or 10d10 psychic. Every ally the party lost across the campaign speaks at once — use the names from your day tracker.
  • 4. Eclipse. Darkness fills the arena for one round. Attacks have disadvantage unless the attacker is in bright light they brought themselves.
  • 5. He stands up. Noctyrix immediately takes a full extra action. Then the order begins again at 1.

Phase two — at 300 HP

  • He steps into his own shadow and pulls the arena in after him. Everyone is now in the Umbra: the ground is the standing stones seen from underneath, all light is dim, and the party's shadows fight beside him — each character faces a shadow of themselves, AC 18, 60 HP, using that character's attacks at half damage.
  • Noctyrix's Cast Shadows, if destroyed, no longer reform. He has spent them getting here.
  • Bright light brought into the Umbra still works, and works better: within bright light, the character's own shadow-double cannot act.

Legendary Actions (3)

  • Step (1). Shadow Step, then one Unmaking Touch.
  • Command the Cast (1). One Aspect takes its turn immediately.
  • Take the Light (2). One creature holding a light source: DC 24 Cha or the light goes out and cannot be relit for one minute.

Minion waves

  • At the start of rounds 1, 4, and 7, eight shadow cultists (AC 15, 30 HP) arrive at the arena's edge. Break Ranks, Necrotic Tempest, and Gravity Squeeze all exist to answer this — and the Necrotic Tempest turns each wave into allies.
  • Every Fallen nation adds a second wave of its own corrupted forces on the same rounds. This is where losing the map is felt in the fight rather than merely announced.

Section 13 — Give them this

The Player Handout

A separate document, deliberately. Hand a player this kit and they can read the night table, three statblocks, every corruption effect, and the Long Dark's true name. The handout is its own page with its own link, and it holds one copy of the rules so the two documents can't drift apart.

What's on it

  • The daily loop, and the resolution ladder — 10+, 25+, natural 20 and 1, Push, Assist, stacking, and the long rest.
  • The three tiers of creative leverage, so they know a good spell is worth something before they ask.
  • All six activities with their skills, including Raise or Reinforce an Army.
  • Garrison orders and Muster, both with the one-per-round and two-per-round caps stated plainly.
  • The Colossus and the Standing Army, with their full order lists.
  • The allied nations, one line each — and the rule that their powers work in any fight but are mostly once ever.
  • Seven things they already know about the war, including that Illva is already going and that the shadow will offer them real help.

What's deliberately off it

  • The night table. They should learn its shape by living through it.
  • The Shadow track's numbers and every corruption effect. They know nations degrade by degrees; they don't know the thresholds.
  • Lieutenant names, statblocks and threat tracks. Names are the reward for Intel — don't spend them on a handout.
  • Noctyrix's statblock, his abilities, and the true name.
  • Which fortification unlock sits at which level. Let them discover that Ward 4 trivialises a whole encounter.
  • Taint. The handout only carries the tease — "the shadow will offer you help; it will be real help." Explain the mechanics the first time somebody takes one, and not before. If you'd rather be upfront, add it yourself; the seduction just works better as a surprise.
  • The workshop list. That's its own printed sheet, one per player — print Section 10 and cut the Answers to Noctyrix table off the bottom, which is DM-only.

Say these three aloud on Day 1, whatever the handout says

  • "You get twelve days. There are no extra days."
  • "Your allies' powers work in any fight, and most of them are once, ever."
  • "One garrison order a round, and two Muster a round. However much you build."

People skim handouts. These three are the ones that shape every decision they make for the rest of the campaign, so they get said out loud as well as printed.

Printing

The handout prints to about two pages on its own. Alongside it you want one workshop list per player and the three blank dossier sheets where everyone can see them. That's the whole table-facing paperwork.
